Where to invest: irrigation and roads for a resilient food supply chain

Problem: limited budgets and many candidate sites. Which mix of irrigation and road upgrades lets Zambia meet 2050 urban food demand at the lowest cost?

  • Scored every 5 km grid cell for irrigation and road potential using AHP-weighted multi-criteria GIS analysis, then mapped priority areas where both pay off.
  • Built a multi-source, multi-destination linear program (Gurobi) that routes food from farms to 2050 urban demand at minimum travel cost, and compared investment scenarios.

Result: investing in irrigation and roads together cut transport burden by ~30% compared with irrigation alone, and cropland needs by ~15–25% compared with roads alone.

County-to-county food freight flows across the US (2007–2017)

Problem: national freight statistics only cover 132 large regions, which is too coarse to see where goods actually move, or which places the supply chain depends on.

  • Co-developed a model that downscales national freight data to every US county for 7 food commodity groups in 2007, 2012 and 2017.
  • Used logistic regression and a gamma hurdle model to predict which county pairs trade and how much, with linear programming and mass-balance constraints to keep totals consistent with production and consumption.
  • Used multi-criteria decision analysis to find the core hub counties that the network depends on.

Result: an open dataset of 206 million flows (1,200+ downloads, 40+ citations), now used in supply chain chokepoint and risk research. The same hub counties stay central across all three years.

What road upgrades are worth: Zambia's maize and cassava supply chains

Problem: moving staple crops across Zambia is expensive, about 3× US trucking cost per tonne-km. If roads got better, who would gain, and by how much?

  • Built a monthly supply chain model of Zambia's maize and cassava markets: 72 district markets, 2010–2020, covering production, consumption, storage, private trade and exports.
  • Solved market clearing with spatial and seasonal price arbitrage (dynamic partial equilibrium), and modeled government grain purchases with a multi-source, multi-destination linear program.
  • Calibrated road and rail trade costs and checked the model against surveyed market prices, then ran a scenario where domestic transport costs fall by half.

Result: halving transport costs cut transport spending by 41.5% ($287M over 2010–2020), raised domestic trade by 5.5% and maize revenue by 10.4%. Most of the gain went to producers rather than consumers.
